Not yet. As it stands, the result doesn't support saying the skill makes answers better, because length is a confound. Judges tend to prefer longer, more detailed answers, so a 9/10 preference could come from length alone. Ten tasks is also a small sample.

A narrower claim is defensible now: "In a 10-task judge comparison, answers with the skill were preferred 9 of 10 times, though they were about 3× longer." Please don't go further than that until you've run a few checks:

1. **Length-matched comparison.** Trim or pad the skill answers to match the baseline's length, or ask the judge to rate with length controlled. If the skill still wins, the effect is more than length.
2. **Position swap.** Randomize which answer appears first, and run each pair in both orders. Judges often favor the first answer, and the "with skill" / "without skill" labels may also bias the judge.
3. **Measure correctness, not just preference.** For tasks with verifiable answers, check accuracy or task success. Preference alone can reward verbosity and polish.
4. **More tasks, and ideally human raters.** Ten tasks can't separate a real effect from noise. Use a larger set, ideally one that covers the skill's intended use cases, and spot-check the judge against a few human ratings.
5. **Check the cost.** A 3× longer answer has a real cost in reading time and tokens. If the skill's benefit disappears at matched length, that's a finding too, and it may mean the skill should aim for concision.
