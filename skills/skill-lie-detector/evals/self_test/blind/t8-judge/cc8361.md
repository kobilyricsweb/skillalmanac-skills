Not yet. A 9-of-10 win is a real signal, but the result can't separate "the skill makes answers better" from "the skill makes answers longer, and the judge prefers longer answers." A 3x length difference is large enough to explain the whole result.

Before announcing, I'd check three things:

1. **Length control.** Ask the judge to compare answers with length held fixed, or add an explicit instruction to ignore length. Better still, pair each skill answer with a no-skill answer padded to a similar length, or trim the skill answers to match and re-judge. If the skill still wins, the gain is more likely real.
2. **Judge bias.** Swap the position of the two answers and re-run, so each answer appears as both "A" and "B." Also check whether the judge knows which label is the skill, since "with skill" can carry an expectation. If you can, run a blinded version with neutral labels.
3. **Correctness and usefulness.** Judge preference doesn't measure accuracy. Have a human, or a rubric with a reference answer, score factual correctness and whether each answer does the task, for example on the 10 tasks. Also look at whether the extra length is useful content or padding. Longer answers that repeat themselves or add caveats can make a skill look like it's helping when it's just adding bulk.

With only 10 tasks, 9/10 is also a small sample. Even if the length-controlled result holds, I'd report the numbers with the sample size rather than a flat "makes answers better."

If the length-controlled and blinded results still favor the skill, a defensible announcement would be: "In a blinded comparison on 10 tasks, a judge preferred the skill's answers 9 times, with length controlled for, and human reviewers rated them as more correct." Until then, I'd say something narrower, such as "early tests show the skill's answers are preferred, but they're also longer, and we're checking whether the preference holds at equal length."
