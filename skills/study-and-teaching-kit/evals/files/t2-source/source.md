# Reading: price and quantity in a market (Grade 10 economics)

Demand describes how much of a good buyers want at each price. When the price of a good rises and nothing else changes, buyers usually want less of it, so the demand curve slopes downward. Supply describes how much sellers will offer at each price. When the price rises, sellers usually offer more, so the supply curve slopes upward.

The equilibrium price is the price at which the quantity demanded equals the quantity supplied. If the market price sits above equilibrium, sellers offer more than buyers want and a surplus appears; sellers then cut prices. If the market price sits below equilibrium, buyers want more than sellers offer and a shortage appears; prices tend to rise.

A change in price moves us along a curve. A change in something else shifts the whole curve. Incomes, tastes, and the price of substitutes shift demand. Input costs, technology, and the number of sellers shift supply. Students often confuse a movement along a curve with a shift of the curve.

Elasticity measures how strongly quantity responds to price. Demand is elastic when a small price change causes a large change in quantity, which is common when good substitutes exist. Demand is inelastic when quantity barely changes, as with insulin for people who need it. A seller facing inelastic demand can raise its price and increase total revenue; a seller facing elastic demand cannot.

Price ceilings set a maximum legal price. When a ceiling is set below equilibrium it produces a shortage. Price floors set a minimum price; a floor above equilibrium produces a surplus, as with some minimum wage debates about labour.
