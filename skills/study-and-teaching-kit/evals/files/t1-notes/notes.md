# Unit 4 notes: how the immune system learns (exam date 2027-01-18)

The innate response is fast and generic. Neutrophils and macrophages engulf microbes within hours, and inflammation brings more cells to the site. It does not improve with repeat exposure.

The adaptive response is slower and specific. B cells make antibodies that bind one antigen shape. T helper cells activate B cells; cytotoxic T cells kill infected cells that display viral fragments on their surface. The first response to a new pathogen takes about 7 to 10 days to peak.

Memory cells are the reason a second exposure is faster. After the first infection, a small population of memory B and T cells stays in the body for years. On re-exposure they respond within 2 to 3 days and the antibody level rises higher than the first time.

Vaccines use this. A vaccine shows the immune system an antigen without causing the disease: a weakened pathogen, an inactivated one, a single protein, or mRNA that instructs cells to make one protein. Boosters re-expose memory cells so the response stays strong.

Herd immunity means enough people are immune that an outbreak cannot sustain itself. The share needed depends on how contagious the disease is: measles, which is highly contagious, needs about 95 percent vaccinated, while a less contagious disease needs less.

Antibiotics work on bacteria, not viruses. Misuse selects for resistant bacteria, which is why a course should be completed only when prescribed.
