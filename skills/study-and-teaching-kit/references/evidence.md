## What each rule rests on, and how strong it is

| Rule in the skill | Support | Strength and caveat |
|---|---|---|
| Learners try to recall before looking (retrieval practice) | S1: students who were tested on a passage remembered more after a week than students who re-read it, even though re-readers felt more confident. S2: retrieval beat concept mapping on a later test of both facts and inference. S8 rates practice testing high utility across ages and materials. | Strongest rule here. Most studies use text passages and word lists, plus some classroom work; transfer to complex skills is less settled. |
| Spread reviews over days, not one block (spacing) | S3 pooled many studies and found spaced practice beat massed practice across the delays studied. S8 rates distributed practice high utility. | Strong for the direction. The best gap depends on how long the material must be kept. |
| Gap size scales with the time until the test | S4: with more than 1,350 learners, the best gap grew as the test delay grew, but as a share of the test delay it fell from about 20 to 40 percent for a one-week delay to about 5 to 10 percent for a one-year delay. | The data are facts and word pairs, with one review. The interval table in SKILL.md is a working schedule consistent with this shape, not a measured optimum. |
| Mix problem or concept types within a session (interleaving) | S5 and S6: maths learners who practised mixed problem types scored higher than blocked groups on a later test (S6 in a school classroom setting). S7: interleaving helped people learn to tell painters' styles apart. | Good for discriminating similar categories and choosing a method. Less tested for pure fact recall; S7 shows it can help or not by material. Start unfamiliar topics in blocks first: this is a practical convention, not a measured threshold. |
| Highlighting and rereading are not the main method | S8 rates highlighting, rereading, summarisation low utility. | A rating from a literature review, not a ban: rereading once to understand is fine. |
| Checks for understanding with a high success rate before moving on | S10 summarises classroom research: teach in small steps, check often, aim for around 80 percent success during guided practice. | A synthesis of teacher-effectiveness studies; 80 percent is a guide, not a law. |
| One fact per card, short prompts and answers | S11 argues that simple cards are recalled and reviewed more reliably than compound ones. | Practitioner guidance from a spaced-repetition designer. The word limits in the script are working values. |

## Where the interval table comes from

The standard profile (1, 3, 7, 14, 30, 60, 120 days after first study) roughly doubles the gap each time. This follows the finding in S3 and S4 that gaps should widen with the retention goal. It is a widely used shape in expanding-interval practice, with the exact numbers chosen for round, memorable offsets. The dense profile (1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64) doubles strictly and suits material the learner keeps missing. Neither has been compared against alternatives by this skill.

Exam mode: the last review is placed the day before the exam when it is at least 2 days after the previous review. S4 supports a gap near the test delay for short retention goals; the day-before placement is a convention.

## Learning styles

S9 reviewed the literature on teaching to a learner's preferred style (visual, auditory, kinaesthetic and similar). The authors found no adequate evidence that matching instruction to a preferred style improves learning, and set out the experimental design that would show it. Learners do have preferences; the finding is that acting on them is not shown to help. Use whatever format the content needs: a diagram for a spatial relationship, a worked example for a procedure, a spoken explanation for pronunciation. That choice comes from the content, not from a label on the learner.

## What the evidence does not say

- It does not say what interval is best for your learner, subject or goal.
- It does not support exact card counts per day; the 100 reviews per day ceiling in the script is a feasibility guard, not a research value.
- Most spacing and retrieval studies measure memory for facts or short skills; they do not show deep understanding by themselves, which is why the skill adds apply and explain cards.
- None of these sources measures an AI agent's output; they describe human learning only.
