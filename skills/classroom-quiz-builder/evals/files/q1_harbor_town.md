# The Rise and Fall of Port Calder's Herring Trade

*Reading for Grade 7 social studies. Port Calder is a fictional town used for this unit.*

## A town built on one fish

Port Calder was founded in 1764 by eleven families who came for one reason: the herring that gathered in Calder Bay every autumn. For its first forty years the town had no market square and no town hall. It had a harbor, a row of smokehouses, and a single road leading inland. The first public building, finished in 1771, was the salt store, because without salt the catch could not be preserved for the journey to distant cities.

## The boom years

The trade grew slowly until 1823, when the canal to the city of Marrow opened. Before the canal, a barrel of salted herring took nine days to reach Marrow by cart. After it, the trip took two days by barge. Cheaper and faster transport meant that fishermen could sell far more fish, and the fleet grew from 40 boats in 1820 to 210 boats in 1850. Coopers, who made barrels, became the best-paid workers in town after ship captains. At the peak in 1850, about three out of every five working adults in Port Calder had a job connected to herring.

The town council, worried even then about taking too many fish, passed the Net Rule in 1838. It banned nets with a mesh smaller than one inch, so that young herring could escape and grow. Most captains obeyed it because the harbor master inspected nets on the dock and could hold a boat in port for a week.

## What went wrong

Two changes ended the good years. First, in 1861 the council abolished the post of harbor master to save money. The Net Rule stayed on the books, but nobody enforced it, and small-mesh nets returned within a few seasons. Second, steam-powered boats arrived in 1874. A steam boat could fish in weather that kept sailing boats in the harbor, and it could haul a net three times as large.

Catches rose at first, which made the town feel richer than ever. The record catch came in 1879. But the fish being caught were younger and smaller each year, and fewer survived to breed. By 1890 the catch had fallen to less than a quarter of the 1879 record. The last smokehouse closed in 1902.

## After the herring

Port Calder did not disappear. Some families left for Marrow, and the population fell by half between 1880 and 1910. Those who stayed turned to boat repair and, later, to visitors: the old salt store became a museum in 1956. In 1988 the regional government declared Calder Bay a protected breeding area, with no commercial fishing allowed. Herring have been seen in the bay again since the late 1990s, though in small numbers.

Historians point out that the town had the right rule early on. The Net Rule failed not because it was a bad idea, but because the town stopped paying someone to enforce it.
