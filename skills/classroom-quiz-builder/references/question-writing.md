# Writing questions and wrong options

Examples use an invented reading about a town library so that no real lesson is implied.

## The question stem

- Ask one thing. A student who knows the material should be able to answer before looking at the options.
- Put the common words in the stem, not repeated in every option.
- Ask positively. If a negative is needed, write NOT or EXCEPT in capitals and keep the options positive.
- Do not copy a sentence from the material and blank a word; that tests memory of wording. Rephrase the idea.
- No clues from grammar: if the stem ends in "an", every option must start with a vowel sound.
- No absolute words ("always", "never") in options unless the material uses them; students learn that absolutes are usually wrong.

## Three levels

| Tag | The student must | Example stem |
|---|---|---|
| recall | find or remember a stated fact | In which year did the library open its second floor? |
| understand | explain, compare or give the reason the text gives | Why did the library move the children's section downstairs? |
| apply | use a rule or idea from the text in a new situation | A visitor wants to borrow a reference atlas for the weekend. According to the lending rules, what will the librarian say? |

An apply question still has exactly one answer provable from a line in the material. The new situation supplies the case; the text supplies the rule.

## Wrong options

A good wrong option is chosen by a student who half-understood. Sources, best first:

1. Something the material says about a different item (the date of another event, the cause of another effect).
2. A typical misunderstanding of the idea.
3. The reverse of the correct relation (cause and effect swapped).
4. A true statement from the material that does not answer this question.

Rules:

- Same category and same form as the correct answer: all years, all reasons starting "Because", all single nouns.
- Lengths within a few words of each other. Write the correct option first, then match it. If the correct one needs a qualifier, give qualifiers to the others.
- All wrong options must be clearly wrong according to the text, not merely less good.
- No overlapping options (if "more than 10" and "more than 20" both appear, one implies the other).
- Order numbers and dates from smallest to largest unless that would break the planned key; then reorder other questions instead.

## Weak to better

Weak:

> What is true about the library?
> A. It is big.
> B. It opened its second floor in 1998, after the town council approved funding following a two-year campaign by residents.
> C. It is closed.
> D. All of the above.

Problems: vague stem, the correct option is the only long and specific one, two throwaway options, a banned option.

Better:

> In which year did the library open its second floor?
> A. 1988
> B. 1996
> C. 1998
> D. 2004

Each wrong year is another date mentioned in the reading (founding, campaign start, renovation).

## Planning the key

For N questions, give each letter N/4 answers, rounding as evenly as possible, then shuffle so that no letter repeats three times running. Fix this list before writing options, and write each correct answer into its planned slot. Balancing afterwards by swapping options tends to break number ordering and grammar.

## Coverage

Divide the material into its sections. Each section that carries a main idea gets at least one question. No more than two questions on the same paragraph. Check that no question's stem gives away another question's answer.

## The Why line

One line for the teacher: why the answer is right, and why the most tempting wrong option is wrong. This is what makes the key usable for going over the quiz in class.
